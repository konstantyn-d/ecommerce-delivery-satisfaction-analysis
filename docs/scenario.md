# "What if" scenario: what could fixing the problem achieve?

Two improvements are tested against today's share of 1–2 star reviews (14.63%) and average score (4.09).
All numbers come from [`scenario_results.md`](scenario_results.md), generated by
[`notebooks/07_scenario.py`](../notebooks/07_scenario.py).

> **Read this first.** The scenario is an estimate based on observed differences between groups of orders. It is
> not a forecast and not a measured effect. Nobody has run the experiment. The purpose is to compare the
> **size** of two levers and to give management an order of magnitude, with a range that shows how uncertain
> the answer is.

## 1. The two scenarios

| | Scenario A: delivery | Scenario B: order completeness |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | The five states with the most excess late orders reach the national late rate of 6.79% | On-time orders with several items get bad reviews as rarely as on-time single-item orders |
| Orders concerned | 1,079 late orders in Rio de Janeiro, Bahia, Ceara, Espirito Santo and Maranhao that would become on-time orders | 9,008 reviewed on-time orders with two or more items |
| Observed difference used | 1–2 star share: 68.7% for late orders vs 10.6% for on-time orders in these states | 1–2 star share: 24.6% for several-item orders vs 7.5% for single-item orders |
| Why this lever | Late delivery is the strongest driver of bad reviews (Phase 4), and these five states hold 77% of the excess late orders | Review coding (Phase 5): 81% of the bad reviews in this group say that part of the order is missing |

"Excess late orders" of a state = its late orders minus the number it would have at the national late rate.
The five target states have 1,079 of the 1,400 excess late orders of all states above the national rate.

## 2. Assumptions

| # | Assumption | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ASSUMPTION: orders that move from the "bad" group to the "good" group are reviewed like the good group, to the extent given by the case factor | see #2 | Core of the method. It treats an observed difference as if it were, at least partly, a causal effect |
| 2 | ASSUMPTION: share of the observed difference that is a real effect | Low 50%, Base 75%, High 100% | Not measurable in this data. 100% is the upper limit. Anchor: if former late orders were reviewed like slow on-time orders (22+ days: 15.8% bad reviews), 88% of the gap would be realised, so 75% is a cautious base |
| 3 | ASSUMPTION: the national late rate is a realistic target for the five states | 6.79% | It is what the marketplace already achieves on average. Long-distance states may not be able to reach it; see limitations |
| 4 | ASSUMPTION: the share of orders that receive a review does not change | 97.3% of late orders in the target states have a review | Keeps the calculation simple; the effect of this assumption is small |
| 5 | ASSUMPTION: order volume, product mix and customer behaviour stay as in the period January 2017 to August 2018 | — | The scenario is a "same orders, better outcome" comparison, not a projection of the future |
| 6 | ASSUMPTION: the two scenarios are independent and can be added | — | A concerns late orders, B concerns on-time orders: no order is in both |

## 3. Method

```
bad reviews avoided = reviewed orders that move
                      x (1-2 star share of the bad group - 1-2 star share of the good group)
                      x case factor

new 1-2 star share  = (bad reviews today - bad reviews avoided) / reviewed orders
new average score   = average today + reviewed orders that move x (score gap) x case factor / reviewed orders
```

Worked example, scenario A, base case: 1,079 orders × 97.3% reviewed × (68.7% − 10.6%) × 75% = 457 bad
reviews avoided.

## 5. What the scenario says

1. **Fixing late deliveries in the worst states helps, but less than the headline finding suggests.** Bringing
   five states to the national late rate removes about 460 bad reviews (305 to 610), or 3% of all bad reviews.
   The reason is arithmetic: only 1,079 of the 99,092 orders change status.
2. **Order completeness is the larger lever.** It concerns over eight times as many reviewed orders, and the
   estimate is about 1,150 bad reviews avoided (768 to 1,535), 8% of all bad reviews. Even its low case is above the high case
   of scenario A.
3. **Together they would move the 1–2 star share from 14.6% to about 13.0%** (12.4% to 13.5%). Meaningful,
   not transformational.
4. **There is a ceiling.** If every late order in the country were on time and the whole observed difference
   were real, 3,391 bad reviews (23.6%) would disappear and the share would be 11.2%. Three quarters of bad
   reviews are out of reach of any action on late deliveries alone.

## 6. Limitations

- **Not causal.** The method assumes that changing the delivery outcome changes the review. The case factors
  express that uncertainty but are themselves assumptions; they are not estimated from data.
- **Scenario B assumes the cause is fixable.** The review coding indicates incomplete deliveries, but the data
  cannot show whether missing items arrive later in a second parcel or are never sent. The fix (and its cost)
  differs between the two.
- **Scenario A may be optimistic for remote states.** A late rate equal to the national average may be
  unrealistic for long-distance routes. An alternative that is not modelled here is to promise later dates
  on those routes, which lowers the late rate without making delivery faster.
- **No cost side.** The scenario estimates the benefit only. Olist's logistics and seller-management costs are
  not in the data, so no return on investment can be computed.
- **Bad reviews, not money.** The link from reviews to revenue is not established in this data: the repeat
  purchase analysis rests on 103 customers (Phase 4). The scenario is therefore expressed in reviews and score,
  not in BRL.
- **Orders that never arrive are outside both scenarios.** They account for 15.0% of bad reviews (2,153) and
  are a separate lever.
- **One period.** The inputs are averages over 20 months that include three months with unusually high late
  rates. In a calm month the effect of scenario A would be smaller, in a peak month larger.
